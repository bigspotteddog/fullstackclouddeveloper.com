Getting started with AI agents

Priming the Pump

Start with an engineering conversation, not a perfect prompt.

This is the start of the process I describe in The Agent Drives, I Navigate. It’s the approach I use in my own work and the first thing I show teammates who are getting started.

The skeptic who called me

“I tried it and it didn’t work for me. I see you doing it, and I don’t believe what I’m seeing. Show me how you do it.”

What changed

Not his skill. Only how he started the conversation. He went on to help others get going.

One of the biggest AI skeptics at my company called me one day. We paired: I asked what he was working on, had him open Claude Code in that project’s folder, explained how I prime the pump, and fed him a few plain questions. He went on to become one of the best AI engineers at the company.

The real barrier

The barrier usually isn’t the AI

It’s the belief that you need an elaborate prompt, or a full spec, before you’ll get anything useful.

Too much

An hour on the perfect prompt

Heavy effort up front, before the agent has looked at a single line of code.

Too little

A one-liner

Something generic comes back, and the conclusion is “AI doesn’t work.”

Both start in the wrong place.

People either spend an hour crafting the perfect prompt, or they type a one-liner, get something generic back, and decide AI doesn’t work. I use plain, almost naive questions that anyone can ask. No prompt engineering and no spec.

The metaphor

An old hand pump won’t draw water from a dry start.

You pour a little water in first, and then it flows. Working with an AI agent is the same.

Instead of a perfect prompt, I start an engineering conversation. Each round points more clearly at the target.

It’s very much like a conversation with another engineer.

I ask about the existing system, clarify requirements, and talk through constraints. The agent asks questions too. Then I say go, and the go always names its target. The agent implements, tests, and verifies the work.

How far to go

Prime only as much as the work needs

ScopeThe conversationWhat it produces
A taskA few questions, like the exampleEnough shared understanding to do that one thing
A featureA longer discussion of requirements and designTickets the agents can work from
An initiativeA larger discussion, over several sessions if neededA document that outlasts any one session

Prompt engineering spends heavy effort on everything; spec-driven development writes a spec for everything. Priming spends effort in proportion to the work.

Writing it down matters as the work grows. An agent’s memory of a long session gets compressed and details are lost, so a larger discussion ends up as a document or tickets the agent can always go back to. I often build iteratively, each round of conversation sharpening the target.

  • A PR posted on the ticket with the fix
  • Before-and-after results for the ticket’s three examples

The ticket primed the problem; the fleet’s accumulated rules primed the process. The solution was in neither. My part was choosing the work and reviewing the result.

How you know it’s primed

Check before you say go

Before go

“Tell me what you’re about to do and why.”

If it matches what you have in mind, it’s primed. If not, keep talking.

During

The agent asks good questions

It raises something you hadn’t thought of, catching a misunderstanding before any code is written.

Coming back

“What are we working on? What’s next?”

Find out what the agent still remembers before you rely on it.

The agent’s own questions are a good sign. When it asks about something you hadn’t thought of, the conversation is doing its job: catching a misunderstanding before any code is written, not after.

Is it really that simple?

“Does that include zip files?” only sounds naive once someone has thought of it.

Anyone can

Ask plain questions. The conversation teaches you as you go.

Experience does

Spot the gap, bring in the constraint, know when the agent’s understanding matches yours, and notice when its work is wrong.

The skeptic

Wasn’t missing the skill, just permission to talk to the agent normally.

When I look back at what I ask the agent, I think almost anyone could do this. But not everyone would ask those particular questions. Experience is what turns plain questions into the right ones. My skeptical colleague was already an experienced engineer, which is probably why he caught on so fast.

Try it

Five steps, starting today

01

Pick one small task you’re actually working on.

02

Open your agent in that project’s folder.

03

Ask plain questions until it understands the system, the gap and the target.

04

Say go, and name the target.

05

Check the result in the running system, not just the tests.

Feel free to ask anything along the way. Worrying about cost held me back most. Once I switched to open-weight models, I felt free to ask for anything, and that freedom was good for learning. A low-cost subscription such as OpenCode Go, about $10 a month, with an agent like Pi goes a long way; right now I’d use glm-5.3-flash. Prices and models change quickly, so check what’s current.

Don’t spend your time building perfect prompts.

Build shared understanding through engineering conversation, as much as the work needs, then delegate and verify.
